Personalising

Personalising your return gifts

One personal touch is the difference between a gift that was chosen and one that was merely ordered. A practical guide to personalising return gifts, even a few hundred of them.

You do not need to personalise everything, and you should not try. The aim is not decoration; it is a single, clear signal that this gift was made for your day and no one else's. One well-placed touch does that. Six competing ones just look busy.

Start with the tag

The simplest and most effective place to personalise is a small tag: a name, a date, your two names, a monogram, or a single line of your own. Keep it short and let it be legible. A pressed or handwritten tag on good paper reads as considered in a way a printed sticker never will. If you want it to feel of a piece with the gift, letterpress in a quiet colour beats anything glossy.

Personalise the palette, not just the paper

The most underused kind of personalisation is colour. A gift woven to your wedding palette, or to the colours a family loves, feels bespoke before a single word is added. Pick one or two tones and carry them through the band, the tassel and the cloth. It is a quiet, powerful way to make hundreds of gifts unmistakably yours.

Match the filling to the moment

Personalisation is not only visual. Choosing a filling that suits the occasion, or nods to the family, is its own kind of thoughtfulness: the couple's favourite sweet, a festival's particular offering, a gentle keepsake for a naming day. The gift that reflects the moment it came from always lands harder than a generic one. Our guide to the fillings goes deeper on this.

Doing it at scale

The trick to personalising a few hundred gifts without a month of work is to fix most of it and vary one thing. Keep a repeated motif, palette and format across the whole batch, with a single variable element, usually the name or the table, changed by hand. It is how a large order stays coherent and still feels individual. We add these touches during finishing, before anything ships.

Know when to stop

Restraint is part of the craft. A name and a date, done well, is plenty. Resist the urge to add a monogram and a ribbon and a message and a photograph all at once; each addition dilutes the one before it. Personal is not the same as crowded.
