What goes inside

The basket is only half the gift. A practical guide to filling it well: the simple formula that rarely fails, ideas by taste, how much to put in, and the few things worth leaving out.

A return gift is really two decisions. The first is the thing that carries it. The second, the one people agonise over, is what goes inside. It gets much simpler once you have a formula.

Something to keep, something to enjoy

Almost every gift that lands well is built from two halves: one thing meant to be kept, and one thing meant to be enjoyed and finished. A small brass dish and a box of sweets. A woven coaster and a bag of filter coffee. The keepsake is what remembers the day after the treat is gone; the treat is what makes the gift feel warm rather than worthy. Get that balance and the rest is detail.

Keepsakes that actually get used

The test for the lasting half is simple: will it be used, or will it become clutter? The pieces that survive are small and genuinely useful. A brass betel-leaf dish for keys or rings. A folded length of Madras cotton that becomes a napkin. A set of coasters. A candle, a bar of good soap, a few seeds to plant. Skip anything purely decorative that has nowhere to live.

Something sweet, with an eye on the calendar

The edible half is where regional character comes in: traditional sweets like adhirasam and Mysore pak, dry fruits, a good chocolate, filter-coffee sachets. Two practical notes. Watch shelf life against your delivery date, so nothing is packed too early. And keep a couple of options for guests who avoid sugar or dairy, since jaggery-based and nut-based sweets travel and keep well.

The blessing layer

For a traditional gift, keep a little turmeric and kumkum, and a betel leaf with areca nut. It costs almost nothing, and it is what turns a nice hamper into a proper South Indian offering. Leaving it out is the most common way a generous gift ends up feeling incomplete.

Two considered things, packed with room to breathe, beat a basket crammed to the lid.

How much to put in

Less than you think. The instinct is to fill a basket to the brim, but a crammed gift reads as anxious rather than generous. Two or three considered things, arranged with a little space around them, look far more expensive than six squeezed in. Match the amount to the vessel, and let the weave show.

Inside the basket

Keep the packaging as honest as the gift. Kraft-paper boxes with a window, a folded cloth, paper tags: nothing plastic, nothing that will be torn off and binned. The wrapping should be part of what is kept, not the first thing thrown away.

What to leave out

A few things reliably cause trouble. Anything very perishable that will not survive a warm week in transit. Loose powders or liquids that can spill through a woven bag. And, for certain occasions, the items custom quietly avoids, sharp objects and the like, which we cover in our guide to gifting by occasion.
