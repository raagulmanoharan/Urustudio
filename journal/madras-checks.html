Madras checks, the cloth we wrap in

The plainest thing in the basket is a folded square of checked cotton. It has been worn at Nigerian funerals and sold as luxury on Madison Avenue. A short history of the cloth we wrap in.

Open one of our baskets and you will nearly always find a folded square of checked cotton lining it. It looks like the most ordinary thing in the gift. It is, in fact, one of the most travelled textiles on earth: a cloth woven a few hours up the coast from where our reed is cut, that has dressed Kalabari funerals in the Niger Delta, become a summer uniform on the American east coast, and been argued over by merchants for three hundred years.

Born at Fort St George

The English East India Company founded Madras in 1639 on a thin strip of the Coromandel coast, chosen for one reason: cloth. The weavers there worked yarn-dyed checks and stripes so fine, and so much cheaper than elsewhere, that the Company built Fort St George to trade in them and drew the region's weavers in with tax breaks. The checked cotton that left the fort in bales carried its origin in its later names. In West Africa, where Portuguese and then British ships took it, it became known as "George cloth," after the fort it shipped from.

The "Real" in Real Madras

By the nineteenth century the cloth had a formal name: the Real Madras Handkerchief. It travelled in bolts about eight yards long, made to be cut into three squares, and that odd first word was a merchant's defence. "Real" meant woven by hand in South India, on a pit loom, from local cotton, as against the cheaper Manchester mill copies then flooding the market. The name was an argument about authenticity, woven into the cloth itself.

A cloth the world renamed

Few textiles have been loved so far from home. Among the Kalabari people of the Niger Delta, Madras has been a prestige cloth since roughly the 1500s, worn from cradle to grave and central to funerals. They gave it their own name, injiri, which is simply their pronunciation of the two words stamped on the trader's bolt: "real India." Kalabari women made it theirs in the most literal way, lifting single threads out of the weave with a needle and cutting them away with a razor to open lace-like patterns, a technique called pelete-bite that the scholar Joanne Eicher spent a career recording. An Indian import became, thread by cut thread, an emblem of Kalabari identity.

Injiri: their word for the cloth, and their pronunciation of the two words stamped on it, "real India."

Guaranteed to bleed

Then, in 1958, an American importer named William Jacobson came to Bombay and was sent to a textile man called C. P. Krishnan Nair, who sold him lightweight, vegetable-dyed madras at about a dollar a yard. Ten thousand yards went to Brooks Brothers in New York. There was only one catch, and Nair had warned of it: the natural dyes were loosely fixed, and the colours would run unless the cloth was hand-washed cold. The care instructions never reached the customers, who put their new plaid in the machine and watched the indigo bleed across everything. What might have been a scandal became the most famous thing about the cloth. An American campaign, remembered for the line "guaranteed to bleed," sold the fading as the whole point, and through the Ivy League summers of the 1960s a fabric first woven for a hot Indian coast became the uniform of the American preppy.

Still on the loom

Through every reinvention, the weaving never left home. It carried on in the clusters of the Coromandel and its hinterland, around Kurinjipadi and Chirala and Nagapattinam, and across the wider handloom belt of Madurai, Erode, Karur and Salem, much of it now held together by cooperatives like Tamil Nadu's Co-optex against the steady pressure of the powerloom. The pattern is still built the honest way, warp and weft dyed before weaving so the check is woven in rather than printed on, indigo crossing turmeric to make the soft greens, madder and iron giving the rust reds, every length a little irregular.

Why we fold it in

We use Madras cotton for the reason those weavers always trusted it: the material is the ornament. We keep to a quiet palette, oatmeal and faded indigo and terracotta, no loud motif, just the grid of the weave and the honest slubs of hand-loomed cotton. Folded into a basket it does two jobs at once. It cushions the gift, and it is a keepsake of its own, a napkin or a wrap or a coaster that will outlast the occasion by years. Like everything we make, it is chosen to be used, not admired once and folded away.
