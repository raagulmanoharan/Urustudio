A short guide to gifting by occasion

Weddings are only the beginning. A guide to the days worth marking, what South Indian families traditionally send their guests home with, and the small etiquette that turns an object into a blessing.

The return gift is not a wedding invention. In South India almost any day that gathers your people carries the same instinct: no one should leave empty-handed. What changes from occasion to occasion is what goes inside, and the etiquette around it is older and more particular than it first looks. Here is how we think about filling a basket for each, and the few quiet rules that hold across all of them.

The blessing layer

Start with what never changes. A little turmeric and kumkum, and a betel leaf with areca nut, are the base of almost every South Indian gift, the layer that turns an object into an offering. A married woman, a sumangali, is honoured with them as she leaves, because she is regarded as a living form of Lakshmi, and the courtesy is extended to every guest without choosing between them. Keep this even when the gift is grand. Without it, the most expensive hamper still feels unfinished.

Weddings and receptions

The full thamboolam, at scale. Betel and areca, a coconut, turmeric and kumkum, jasmine for the hair, and a tier of sweets. Behind the guest gifts sits seer, the older exchange between the two families, whose trays of silk, sweets, fruit and coins are counted out in auspicious sets. Quantities for guests stretch from fifty close family to a few thousand at a reception, and the count is usually kept generous rather than exact, because running short is the thing a host most fears. Woven to your palette, filled to your number.

Gruhapravesam, the housewarming

A new house is blessed before it is lived in, and the guests who come to bless it are sent home with something for their own. Reach for the objects that belong to the ritual: a small brass lamp, a few mango leaves, turmeric and kumkum, a sweet to share on the first evening under the roof. Useful, homely, quietly auspicious. One caution is particular to this day: avoid anything that reads as an ending. No sharp knives or scissors, and skip pens and towels. Give a lamp, not a blade.

Festivals and poojas

During Navaratri, married women walk from house to house to see each other's golu, the tiered display of dolls, and no one leaves without a thamboolam and a cup of sundal. Varalakshmi Vratham and Diwali keep the same courtesy. The filling is seasonal: a brass diya, incense, turmeric and kumkum, a blouse piece, the sweets the festival asks for, and often a few glass bangles. There is a small grace even to receiving it, the guest lifting the contents from the tray with her right hand and raising them briefly to her eyes before they are bagged. The only real difficulty is having it ready in time for a calendar that never moves.

Valaikaappu and naming

For a baby shower in the seventh or ninth month, the hero is unmistakable: glass bangles, slid onto the mother's wrists in odd numbers, red on one arm and green on the other, because the sound of them is believed to reach the child. Around them go turmeric and kumkum, a soft cloth, a small silver keepsake meant to be grown into. The register is gentle and the palette tender, but the instinct is the same. Send them home with something kept.

Corporate and bulk

The newest occasion, and the one most often done badly. Volume tempts everyone toward the generic hamper. It need not. Dry fruit and mithai in a clean, reusable box, a coaster set, a handwritten card: the same care as a wedding gift, simply repeated a few hundred times. A small thing worth doing is to mark Pongal, not only Diwali. The regional courtesy is noticed.

Restraint reads as expense. Specificity reads as care.

A few quiet rules

Whatever the occasion, the grammar holds. Favour reds and greens and golds, and keep black and stark white out of the wrapping, since both carry the wrong associations. Round a cash gift up by a single rupee, to 101 or 501, so the blessing cannot be evenly divided. Avoid the gifts custom warns against: anything sharp, footwear, leather. And where you can, make one thing personal, a name, a date, a line of your own, because that is the difference between a gift that was chosen and one that was merely ordered.
