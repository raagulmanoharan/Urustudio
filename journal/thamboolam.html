Traditions

Thamboolam, and why the gift outlasts the day

The little bundle a South Indian family presses into every guest's hands on the way out, and why we think it should last longer than the day it was given.

In a Tamil home, no one leaves a celebration empty-handed. The thamboolam is the small parcel you are given at the door: betel leaves and areca nut, a coconut, a pinch of turmeric and kumkum, something sweet. It is a blessing you can hold, a way of saying thank you for standing with us today.

A gift that used to disappear

For a long time the vessel that carried it was an afterthought. A plastic tray, a printed cover, a bag that went straight into the bin by the next morning. The gesture was generous. The packaging was forgettable.

We started Uru to fix the second half of that sentence. If the blessing is meant to last, the thing that carries it should too.

A gift from your day should outlast it.

The same bundle, made to keep

Our baskets hold the same thamboolam a family has always given, arranged the way you would arrange it at home. Handwoven korai reed, an ivory cotton band, a folded length of Madras cotton, a brass dish for the betel and nut. When the sweets are gone, the basket stays. It carries fruit to a neighbour, holds keys by the door, sits on a shelf and remembers the day.

That is the whole idea. Keep the tradition exactly as it is. Change only the thing it comes in.

Made to order in the weeks before your day. Tell us the date and we will shape the gift.
